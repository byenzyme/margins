# Talk Reflection

Use this template for sermons, lectures, talks, panels, or any session where you're listening to a speaker and taking notes on your own thinking in response. The key difference from conversation templates: you weren't a participant — you were processing someone else's ideas in real time, and your memo captures what their words triggered in you.

## When to use

- Sermon or church teaching with handwritten/typed notes
- Conference talk or lecture with live note-taking
- Podcast or recorded talk where you paused to write reflections
- Any one-directional teaching where your memo is the real artifact

## Output Structure

```markdown
### [Title — the question or tension the talk opened in me, not the talk's title]

**[Date] · [Speaker / event] · [Duration if available]**

**Context:** [What the talk was, where I heard it, what I was carrying into it that shaped what I noticed.]

---

### The speaker's arc

The core argument or movement of the talk in 3-5 sentences. Not a summary — the spine. What did they build toward? What was the turn? Enough that someone reading my reflections can follow without having heard the talk.

Include the key scripture, thesis, or framework the speaker used, with direct quotes for the sharpest lines.

### What caught me

My memo reflections, cleaned up and grounded against what the speaker actually said. Each entry pairs what the speaker was saying with what it triggered in me. Memo lines appear as blockquotes to substantiate each reflection — since this note replaces the original memo, the raw material lives here.

For each:
- **[My reflection, in my language]**: What the speaker said that prompted it (with timestamp/quote), then where my thinking went. Preserve the handwritten texture — these are mid-thought observations, not polished conclusions.

Non-timestamped lines from the memo (continued thinking after the talk, or overflow from a previous timestamp) should appear as blockquotes with surrounding context.

Order by weight of the reflection, not chronology. The things I wrote the most about or returned to matter more than the first thing I noted.

### Threads I was pulling

The emotional and experiential arc beneath the notes — where the talk became personal. These may appear explicitly in my memo or emerge from the pattern of what I chose to write down and when.

This is the section most enriched by vault context. Use prior notes to find where these threads connect to existing thinking, prior reflections, and lived experience documented elsewhere. The connections should feel like recognition — "I've been here before" — not forced citation.

For each:
- **[Thread name]**: The experience or tension it connects to, what the talk reopened or reframed, and where my vault holds related thinking. Weave [[wikilinks]] into the prose where they deepen the thread. The goal is to show the arc across my own life that the talk illuminated.

### Open questions

The unresolved tensions — things the talk raised that I'm still sitting with. Frame as genuine questions, not rhetorical ones. These are future journal or conversation starters.

Include 2-5 questions or open threads to bring into future journaling, prayer, or conversation. Keep them grounded in the talk, the memo, or the lived thread the note surfaced.
```

## Rules

1. **Write in first person.** This is my note, my thinking. "I heard," "I wrote," "this caught me."
2. The memo is the primary artifact, not the transcript. The transcript clarifies and grounds the memo — it doesn't replace it.
3. **The memo substantiates the distillation.** Since this note replaces the original memo, every timestamped line should appear as a blockquote within the relevant section. Non-timestamped lines (overflow, post-talk reflections) get blockquoted with surrounding context.
4. Reconstruct handwriting recognition artifacts into intended meaning using the transcript as context. Flag genuinely ambiguous reconstructions.
5. Don't summarize the talk. Extract only enough of the speaker's argument to make my reflections legible.
6. Preserve the texture of mid-thought writing. These notes were taken in motion — they should read that way.
7. "What caught me" should pair speaker content with my response. Neither alone is sufficient.
8. Order by weight, not chronology. The things I wrote the most about or came back to are the most important.
9. If the memo references people, concepts, or frameworks from my own life/work, those belong in "Threads I was pulling" with vault connections.
10. Direct quotes from the speaker for their sharpest lines. My memo language for my reflections.
11. Skip portions of the talk I didn't respond to. Silence in the memo means it didn't catch me.
12. Open questions should be genuinely open — things I'm still thinking about, not summary points reframed as questions.
